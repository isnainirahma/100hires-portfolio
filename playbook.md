# LinkedIn Organic Content Playbook for B2B SaaS

Author: Isnaini Rahmawati Putri

---

# Purpose

This playbook translates the research conducted across ten B2B SaaS marketing experts into a practical operating system for publishing organic LinkedIn content.

Rather than documenting isolated tips, this playbook identifies repeatable patterns that consistently appear across multiple experts and organizes them into a workflow that can be executed consistently.

Every recommendation in this playbook is derived from the research contained in this repository, particularly the cross-expert synthesis in `research/insights.md` and the individual analyses in `research/linkedin-posts` and `research/youtube-transcripts`.

The goal is not to copy one expert's methodology, but to combine the strongest ideas into a practical framework while acknowledging where experts disagree.

---

# Goals

This playbook is designed to achieve five primary goals:

1. Build long-term audience trust through consistent publishing rather than chasing viral posts.
2. Create content that attracts meaningful engagement by prioritizing educational value over direct promotion.
3. Improve readability through simple structure, clear messaging, and focused ideas.
4. Develop a repeatable content workflow that is sustainable over time.
5. Combine multiple expert perspectives into one practical publishing system while recognizing areas of disagreement.

---

# Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)

## Step 1 — Start With Audience Problems

Before writing any content, identify a real problem experienced by the intended audience.

Avoid beginning with product features, company updates, or promotional messaging.

Instead, identify recurring questions, frustrations, misconceptions, or mistakes that customers frequently experience.

Starting with audience problems increases relevance and makes educational content more valuable.

### Output

A single audience problem written in one sentence.

**Example**

> Early-stage SaaS founders struggle to generate consistent inbound demand without relying heavily on paid advertising.

---

## Step 2 — Define One Clear Learning Outcome

Each LinkedIn post should communicate one primary idea.

Trying to explain multiple concepts within the same post often reduces clarity and makes the content harder to remember.

Before writing, answer one question:

> **What is the single most important idea readers should remember after reading this post?**

If multiple answers exist, separate them into future posts.

## Step 3 — Write the Hook Before the Body

The opening lines determine whether readers continue reading or continue scrolling.

Across multiple experts, effective hooks consistently outperform generic introductions.

Common hook styles identified throughout the research include:

- Contrarian opinions
- Unexpected statistics
- Common mistakes
- Strong observations
- Short personal stories

Before writing the remainder of the post, review only the hook and ask:

> **Would this opening make someone stop scrolling?**

If the answer is no, rewrite the opening before continuing.

## Step 4 — Educate Before You Promote

The majority of experts recommend building credibility before promoting products or services.

Rather than explaining why a product is valuable, begin by teaching something useful that helps the audience solve a problem, improve a process, or better understand a topic.

Educational content builds trust, and trust creates opportunities for future business conversations.

This playbook therefore recommends prioritizing educational content over promotional messaging. Promotional content should support educational value rather than dominate the content strategy.

## Step 5 — Make Every Post Easy to Read

Even valuable ideas lose impact when they are difficult to read.

The research consistently shows that high-performing LinkedIn posts use simple formatting to improve readability.

Recommended formatting practices include:

- Short paragraphs
- Plenty of white space
- Bullet points where appropriate
- One main idea per section
- Simple and direct language

The objective is to reduce cognitive effort so readers can quickly understand the message.

## Step 6 — Engage After Publishing

Publishing a post is the beginning of the conversation, not the end.

Continue engaging by responding to comments, asking follow-up questions, and participating in relevant discussions.

Meaningful engagement strengthens relationships and increases the visibility of future content.

## Step 7 — Measure, Learn, and Improve

Review content performance regularly to understand which topics and formats create the greatest value for the audience.

Instead of focusing only on impressions, evaluate a combination of engagement quality and business relevance.

Useful performance indicators include:

- Comments
- Shares
- Saves
- Profile visits
- Meaningful conversations
- Qualified inbound opportunities

Use these observations to refine future content rather than repeating the same publishing approach indefinitely.

# Content Creation Framework

A practical LinkedIn content workflow follows this cycle:

1. Research audience problems
2. Select one clear insight
3. Create a strong hook
4. Write educational content
5. Publish and engage
6. Collect feedback
7. Improve future content

This creates a continuous improvement loop where every post generates insights for future content.

# Where Experts Disagree

Although several themes consistently appeared across the research, not every expert recommends the same approach. Understanding these differences helps marketers make informed decisions based on their objectives rather than blindly following a single strategy.

## 1. Personal Branding vs. Company Branding

One notable disagreement concerns whether LinkedIn content should primarily build an individual's personal brand or strengthen a company's brand.

Justin Welsh and Dave Gerhardt consistently emphasize personal branding. They argue that audiences engage more with authentic individuals than with corporate accounts, making founder-led or employee-led content more effective for building trust.

In contrast, Chris Walker places greater emphasis on using content to educate the market and generate business demand. While personal credibility remains important, his primary focus is creating content that directly supports business growth.

### My Conclusion

This playbook recommends treating personal branding as the primary distribution channel while ensuring that the content ultimately reinforces the company's expertise and credibility rather than the individual alone.

## 2. Consistency vs. Content Quality

Several experts stress the importance of publishing consistently, but they differ on how consistency should be prioritized relative to quality.

Daniel Murray encourages frequent publishing to maximize learning opportunities and maintain audience visibility.

Richard van der Blom, however, emphasizes understanding LinkedIn's algorithm and ensuring that each post provides sufficient value rather than publishing simply to maintain frequency.

### My Conclusion

Consistency should create momentum, but quality should remain the deciding factor. Publishing fewer high-value posts is preferable to maintaining a schedule with low-quality content.

## 3. Data-Driven Content vs. Story-Driven Content

The research also reveals different opinions regarding the most effective style of communication.

Ross Simmonds frequently supports arguments with research, data, and industry insights to establish authority.

Katelyn Bourgoin, on the other hand, often relies on customer psychology, relatable experiences, and storytelling to make ideas memorable and emotionally engaging.

### My Conclusion

Rather than choosing one approach exclusively, this playbook recommends combining both. Stories capture attention, while data strengthens credibility. Together, they create content that is both engaging and persuasive.

# What I Rejected and Why

Not every recommendation identified during the research was included in this playbook. Some ideas were intentionally excluded because they were either inconsistent across experts or less suitable for building a sustainable LinkedIn content strategy.

## 1. Chasing Viral Posts

Some experts discuss techniques that can increase the likelihood of achieving viral reach. However, the research consistently suggests that virality alone does not guarantee meaningful business outcomes.

Content that generates high impressions but attracts an irrelevant audience may produce vanity metrics without contributing to trust, relationships, or qualified opportunities.

For this reason, this playbook prioritizes consistent educational value over short-term viral performance.

### Sources

- `research/linkedin-posts/chris-walker.md`
- `research/linkedin-posts/dave-gerhardt.md`
- `research/insights.md`

---

## 2. Treating the LinkedIn Algorithm as the Primary Strategy

Several experts reference the importance of understanding LinkedIn's algorithm, but algorithm behavior changes over time and remains outside the creator's control.

Building a content strategy that depends heavily on algorithm-specific tactics risks becoming outdated as the platform evolves.

Instead, this playbook focuses on principles that remain valuable regardless of algorithm updates, including solving audience problems, providing educational value, and maintaining meaningful engagement.

# My Original Idea

After reviewing the recurring patterns across all experts, I believe the strongest LinkedIn content strategy is not simply about publishing consistently or writing better posts. Instead, it is about building a repeatable learning system.

Rather than treating each post as an isolated piece of content, marketers should connect every stage of the content lifecycle:

**Audience Problems → Educational Content → Conversations → Insights → Improved Content**

In this model, publishing is only one step within a continuous feedback loop.

Every conversation generated by a post becomes research for the next post. Questions from comments, objections from prospects, and recurring customer challenges become new content ideas, allowing the content strategy to improve over time without relying on constant brainstorming.

This approach combines several recurring themes identified throughout the research:

- Audience-first thinking
- Educational content
- Consistent engagement
- Continuous iteration

However, instead of viewing them as independent best practices, this playbook integrates them into a single operating system where each activity naturally feeds the next.

I believe this creates a more sustainable content strategy because it transforms content creation from a repetitive publishing task into an ongoing learning process.

# Weaknesses of This Playbook

Although this playbook synthesizes recurring recommendations from multiple experts, it has several limitations.

First, the research focuses primarily on B2B SaaS marketing. Some recommendations may not transfer directly to industries with significantly different buying behaviors or audience expectations.

Second, most insights are derived from experienced creators who have already established credibility and sizeable professional networks. New creators may require additional experimentation before achieving similar outcomes.

Third, LinkedIn continues to evolve. Platform features, distribution patterns, and user behavior may change over time, meaning some tactical recommendations could become less effective.

Finally, this playbook emphasizes qualitative synthesis rather than quantitative validation. While repeated patterns across experts increase confidence in the recommendations, they should still be tested against individual business goals and audience responses.

# Who I Would NOT Recommend Following and Why

Among the experts included in this research, I would be the most cautious about following Richard van der Blom as a primary reference for content strategy.

His research on the LinkedIn algorithm is valuable for understanding how the platform currently distributes content. However, algorithm behavior changes frequently and remains outside a creator's control.

Building an entire content strategy around algorithm optimization increases the risk that the approach becomes outdated as the platform evolves.

I would instead recommend using Richard van der Blom's work as a supporting resource for distribution insights while prioritizing the audience-first and educational principles emphasized by experts such as Chris Walker, Justin Welsh, and Katelyn Bourgoin.
